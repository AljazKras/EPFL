## Lecture 1
**Summary**
 - Our example state of a system of $N$ atoms in $\mathbb{R}^3$ lives in a $6N$-dimensional space, consisting of the position $r(x,y,z)$ and momentum $p(p_x,p_y,p_z)$ of each atom, but conservation laws reduce this to $6N-4$: $-1$ because energy is constant and $-3$ because the total linear momentum is conserved along each of the three spatial directions.
 
 - The Hamiltonian is defined *H(r,p)*= *K(*p*)* + *V*(*r*), where K(*p*) is the kintetic and *V*(*r*) potential energy, we then differentiated it with respect to time to time and inserting Hamilton's equations$\frac{dH(r,p)}{dt}$ and showed that the total energy is conserved.

 -  The macroscopic properties of a system are defined by its microstate, but not just by a single one. Atomic motion is orders of magnitude faster than a macroscopic measurment, an observable catches an average over an enormous amount of microstates $\bar{A} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^TA\,dt$, where ${A}$ is the microscopic observable at a given time, and $T$ is the duration of the measurement.

**Question**
Since the trajectory keeps changing (it has nonzero $\ddot{x}$, $\overset{\ldots}{x}$, …),  why don't these higher derivatives need to be part of the state?
**Answer**
The definition of the state is separate from the definition of its time evolution. Once you have positions and momenta and the evolution equations, you can compute all higher order derivatives of, e.g. the position, via the equations.
